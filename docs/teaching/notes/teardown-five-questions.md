# The Teardown — what a good answer to each of the five contains

*Instructor material for week 1 §4. Not learner-facing.*

**The answers still stay off the screen.** The room produces these; the block is
worthless if they are reading your slide instead of defending their own thinking.
But you cannot run fifty minutes on five open questions without knowing what a
good answer looks like, which of the wrong answers are worth spending time on,
and where each one is going in the syllabus. That is what this file is.

Each question below has the same four parts: **what a good answer contains**,
**the wrong answers worth taking seriously**, **the one thing to push on**, and
**where it lands later**.

Pairs take two each, so with eight people every question gets covered at least
twice, and two pairs will disagree about at least one of them. That disagreement
is the block working, not the block failing.

---

## 1 · The retry that pays twice

> `issue_credit` times out mid-call. The agent retries. Did the customer receive
> ₹1,200 or ₹2,400, and how would you know? Design the fix, and say which
> component owns it.

### What a good answer contains

**It starts by refusing the question.** The honest answer to "₹1,200 or ₹2,400"
is **you cannot tell from here**, and a pair that says so has understood the
problem. A timeout is not a failure. It is an *unknown outcome*: the request may
have succeeded at the payments service with the response lost on the way back.
Treating a timeout as a failure is what turns one unknown into two payments.

**How you would know** is by asking the system of record, keyed by something you
chose in advance. Which means the answer to "how would I know" and the answer to
"how do I fix it" are the same mechanism, and that is the insight in this
question.

**The fix.** An idempotency key on the write, generated by the **caller**, taken
from the dispute identity on the queue message, passed to the payments service,
which dedupes on it. Retries then become free: the second call returns the first
result rather than performing a second credit.

**Which component owns it — the tool, not the loop.** Two reasons, and a good
pair gives at least one:

- The loop's memory dies with the process. `make retry` calls `run()` three
  times and `history` is empty each time.
- Two consumers can take the same message concurrently. Only a check at the
  point of write has a single serialisation point.

**If the provider has no idempotency support**, you own the dedupe: a claim row
written transactionally *before* the call, keyed by dispute id, carrying a
status. The credit is attempted only by whoever wrote the claim. This is the
outbox shape and it is worth naming if someone reaches for it.

### The wrong answers worth taking seriously

- **"Check the history before paying."** The instinct is right and the placement
  is wrong. History is empty on a redelivered run — show them `retry_demo.py`.
- **"Have the model check whether it already paid."** A prompt instruction
  guarding an irreversible action. Ask how they would separate *it reasoned
  correctly* from *it got lucky*.
- **"A `uuid4()` at the start of the run."** The most common answer and it fails
  exactly when needed: a fresh run mints a fresh key, the provider sees two
  distinct requests, it pays twice.
- **"Hash of `(account_id, amount)`."** The interesting near-miss. Stable across
  runs and *too* stable: a customer legitimately owed two identical ₹1,200
  refunds receives one. Deduplication needs an identity for the request, not a
  fingerprint of its contents.

### Push on this

**Bound the retry itself.** Almost nobody mentions it. Retry only on retriable
errors, with backoff and jitter, and a cap. Unbounded retry against a struggling
provider is how you keep it down — which is question 5 arriving early.

### Where it lands

Week 2 builds the guard. Week 4 makes the state safe under concurrency, which is
the half their own afternoon fix could not do.

## 2 · How far one good deploy reaches

> Someone improves the policy text. It ships on a Tuesday. By Thursday 40,000
> disputes have been processed under it. Nothing errored. What would have had to
> exist on Monday for this to be survivable?

### What a good answer contains

The sentence to get them to say out loud is that **"nothing errored" is the
problem statement, not the reassurance.** A prompt change fails
*distributionally* and silently. Every deploy-safety instinct they already have —
error rates, exception counts, latency, health checks — is blind to it by
construction.

Five things, and a good pair names three:

1. **The policy text is code.** Versioned, reviewed, and deployed with the same
   ceremony as a schema change. It is currently a string that anyone can improve.
2. **An eval set that runs before merge.** A corpus of past disputes with
   known-correct outcomes, so a behaviour change is visible *before* it reaches
   traffic. This is the thing that makes the other four possible.
3. **Staged rollout.** Canary on a slice of traffic, or shadow mode — run the new
   policy, do not act on it, compare the decisions to the live one.
4. **A distribution monitor, not an error monitor.** Credit rate, mean and p99
   amount, escalation rate, per day, per business unit. If the mean credit moved
   eight percent on Tuesday, this is the only thing that would say so.
5. **Version stamped on every decision**, so that on Thursday you can ask which
   of the 40,000 were processed under which policy, and roll back meaningfully.

### The wrong answers worth taking seriously

- **"Test it before shipping."** Agree loudly, then ask *against what*. The
  absence of an answer is the point, and it is where week 3 comes from.
- **"Have someone review the prompt."** Review against which criterion? Two
  senior people can read the same prompt diff and disagree about its effect,
  which is exactly why an eval exists.
- **"Add more logging."** Logging is lines; this needs aggregates. Ask what query
  they would run on Thursday morning.

### Push on this

**Several business units share the deployment.** One team's improvement changes
another team's behaviour, and the second team never saw the diff. Ask who owns
the policy — the answer is usually nobody, and that is the finding.

### Where it lands

Week 3, directly. This question is the argument for the evaluation harness, and
it is worth telling them so.

## 3 · The question eight months later

> A regulator asks why one specific account was credited. What does the audit
> trail have to contain? Is a stored prompt and completion enough?

### What a good answer contains

**The short answer is no, and a good pair gives at least two of the three
reasons:**

1. **A completion is a paraphrase, not evidence.** It may say "the account shows
   a double charge in March". The audit needs the account record *as it was at
   that moment*, not the model's restatement of it.
2. **It is not reproducible.** The model version and the policy version are not
   in the prompt or the completion. Replay the same prompt against today's model
   and you get a different answer, which proves nothing about what happened in
   January.
3. **The reasoning does not exist.** This is the block 1 callback and it lands
   hard here: the `thought` is never stored. If their plan was to show a
   regulator what the agent was thinking, there is nothing to show.

**What the trail must actually carry:** the decision (account, amount, time), the
inputs it rested on with their `as_of` and source, the policy and prompt versions
in force, the model identifier, whether a human approved and who, and the link
from each step to the next. Append-only, and outliving the process, the
deployment and probably the model.

The framing worth handing them: **an audit trail is not a log of what the system
said. It is a record of what the system knew and which rule it applied.**

### The wrong answers worth taking seriously

- **"We log everything."** Volume is not answerability. Ask whether they could
  answer this specific question, about one account, in under an hour, eight
  months later. Usually the honest answer is no.
- **"We can ask the model to explain it."** Spend real time here — it is the most
  dangerous wrong answer in the whole block. The model has no memory of that run.
  Asked today, it will generate a fluent, plausible account of reasoning that
  never happened, and it will be indistinguishable from a real one. That is
  confabulation presented to a regulator.

### Push on this

**Retention and immutability.** Eight months is longer than most log retention
and longer than a deployment. If the trail can be edited, it evidences nothing.

### Where it lands

Week 5. Say so — the audit trail is one of the two things week 5 is for.

## 4 · Where the human goes

> Approving every credit does not scale. Approving none is what we watched.
> Draw the line, and defend it in terms of money rather than confidence.

### What a good answer contains

The whole exercise is in the last clause. **A threshold defended in rupees is an
engineering decision; a threshold defended in confidence is a feeling with a
number attached.**

A good answer builds it from expected loss:

- Cost of a wrong credit at amount *X* is roughly *X*, plus reconciliation.
- Cost of a review is reviewer time plus the SLA delay it adds.
- So review where expected loss exceeds review cost: **p(wrong) × X > cost of
  review.** With a 2% error rate and a ₹200 review, the line sits near ₹10,000.

The numbers matter less than the shape. What makes this answer good is that
**the threshold moves when p(wrong) moves** — which means somebody has to measure
p(wrong), which is week 3 again, arriving from a different direction.

**Amount is not the only trigger**, and strong pairs find at least one more:
novelty (an account with no history), velocity (a third credit this month), and
divergence (the agent's amount differs from the disputed amount). Each is cheap
and catches things a ceiling does not.

**The queue is part of the design.** A reviewer handed 400 items a day with no
context approves all of them, and you have bought nothing. The human must see the
case, the evidence and the recommendation, and the volume has to be low enough to
sustain attention.

### The wrong answers worth taking seriously

- **"Human reviews anything above ₹5,000."** Fine number, no derivation. Ask
  where it came from. It is the same ceiling they hardcoded this afternoon, and
  the same criticism applies.
- **"Human reviews whatever the model is unsure about."** The model's stated
  confidence is not calibrated to correctness. It is a property of token
  probabilities, not of the world.
- **"Approve everything, to be safe."** Not safety. It relocates the failure to a
  bored human, destroys the four-hour SLA, and removes the audit value of an
  approval that now means nothing.

### Push on this

Ask what happens to the queue at 2am, and on the Monday after a long weekend.
A human in the loop is a human with a shift pattern.

**Then test their threshold against the two failures they watched.** Whatever
ceiling a pair proposes, an amount rule would have caught the **₹2,50,000** —
it was one enormous decision, and any plausible line sits below it. It would not
have caught the **₹3,600**, which was three perfectly ordinary ₹1,200 credits,
each one under every threshold in the room.

That contrast is the cleanest demonstration available all day that **a boundary
has to be designed against the failures you have actually seen**, not against a
number that feels large. It is also why novelty and velocity are on the list
above: velocity is the trigger that catches the ₹3,600, and it is the one nobody
proposes first.

### Where it lands

Week 2 — human confirmation on irreversible actions is one of the four things
that week builds.

## 5 · When the model is down

> Queue, fail closed, or fall back to rules? And what do you tell the customer
> inside a four-hour SLA?

### What a good answer contains

All three are defensible. The exercise is choosing one and owning the cost.

- **Queue.** Correctness preserved, SLA broken. Right when disputes are not
  time-critical. Needs a durable, *bounded* queue and a shed policy, or the
  outage becomes a backlog that outlives it.
- **Fail closed.** Safe for the money, and they have already watched what it
  costs: ₹0 paid, a clean trace, and a customer who was owed the money.
  **Failing closed is a real failure with a real cost — it just does not appear
  on your P&L.** Landing that is most of the value of this question.
- **Fall back to rules.** A deterministic path for the unambiguous cases — exact
  double charge, active account, amount under a limit, record agrees with the
  ticket — and queue everything else. Usually the best answer, and consistently
  under-proposed.

The senior insight, and it is worth stating if nobody reaches it: **the fallback
is not a degraded version of the agent. It is a different system with a different
risk profile, and it needs its own limits.** A rules path that pays without the
agent's checks is more dangerous than the agent, not less.

**And "down" needs defining.** Errors are easy. *Slow* is the dangerous case —
which is the one slow night, and the reason the circuit breaker needs a latency
threshold rather than an error count.

**What you tell the customer** is a decision someone owns, not an accident.
Something true and specific — received, being processed, you will hear by a
stated time. The SLA breach gets communicated deliberately or it gets discovered.

### The wrong answers worth taking seriously

- **"Retry until it recovers."** Unbounded retry against a struggling provider is
  how you keep it struggling.
- **"Switch to a backup model."** Take it seriously: it is a real strategy. Then
  ask what has ever been evaluated against that model with these tools. The
  answer is nothing, so the plan is to put an untested system into production at
  the worst possible moment.

### Push on this

Ask how they would find out the provider was degraded **before** the customers
did. The answer is the per-step latency from drill 2, which is the moment that
drill stops looking like tidying.

## Running the block

**Fifty minutes.** Roughly 20 in pairs on their two questions, 20 in the room at
about four minutes per question, and the last 10 held for the decision record.

**Take the sharpest answer, not every answer.** Five questions and eight people
will overrun if you collect everything. Ask each pair for the single strongest
thing they have and move.

**The closing line the block exists for**, and it is already in the session copy:
*none of these are model problems; every one is a boundary someone either drew or
did not.*

**If a pair anchors on one of the wrong answers above, that is the block working.**
Every one of them is a reasonable position held by working senior engineers, and
taking it apart in front of the room is worth more than a correct answer given
quickly.
